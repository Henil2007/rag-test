[DOCUMENT: Micro Finance Scheme Guidelines]
The Micro Finance Scheme is designed for very small rural enterprises with a total project cost up to Rs. 1,40,000. Under this scheme, the funding agency (State Channelizing Agency) provides up to 90% of the project cost as a loan, capped at a maximum of Rs. 1,25,000. The beneficiary must contribute the remaining 10% as margin money. The interest rate charged to the beneficiary is 6.5% per annum, which is a concessional rate compared to standard market lending rates of 12-14%. The repayment tenure is 3 years, and this includes a moratorium period of 3 months during which no principal repayment is required (interest may still accrue). This scheme is best suited for petty trading, small tailoring units, minor food processing, and single-person service businesses.

[DOCUMENT: Term Loan Scheme Guidelines]
The Term Loan Scheme covers larger rural and semi-urban enterprises with a project cost between Rs. 1,40,000 and Rs. 50,00,000. The agency provides up to 90% of project cost as a loan, capped at Rs. 45,00,000. The interest rate is 8% per annum. Repayment tenure is 7 years, including a moratorium period of 6 months. This scheme suits dairy units, textile production units, retail shops with inventory, and small manufacturing setups that require higher capital investment than micro enterprises.

[DOCUMENT: Margin Money Requirement]
Under both schemes, beneficiaries must contribute exactly 10% of the total project cost as margin money (their own capital), with the agency financing the remaining 90% as a concessional loan. This is calculated as: Project Cost = Margin Capital / 0.10, and Loan Amount = Project Cost * 0.90. For example, a beneficiary with Rs. 1,00,000 in available margin capital qualifies for a Rs. 10,00,000 project and a Rs. 9,00,000 loan under the Term Loan Scheme.

[DOCUMENT: Dairy Sector Business Notes]
Dairy micro-enterprises in rural India typically require investment in milch animals, fodder storage, and cold-chain or chilling infrastructure if selling beyond the immediate village. Common risks include seasonal fodder price fluctuation, disease outbreaks among cattle, and dependency on a single milk cooperative or dairy buyer for offtake. Profitability improves significantly when farmers diversify into value-added products like ghee, paneer, or curd rather than selling raw milk alone. Local market saturation for raw milk selling is typically high in villages with an existing dairy cooperative; opportunity is stronger in value-added dairy products and direct-to-consumer models in nearby towns.

[DOCUMENT: Retail Sector Business Notes]
Rural retail (kirana/general stores) enterprises depend heavily on footfall, catchment population within walking/cycling distance (typically 1-3 km in villages, 3-5 km in blocks), and competition density. A block with more than 4-5 existing general stores per 1000 households is considered saturated for the standard grocery format; opportunity shifts toward specialty retail (agri-inputs, mobile recharge and electronics, ready-made garments) in such areas. Threats include dependency on wholesale supply chains, seasonal cash flow tied to agricultural harvest cycles, and price competition from larger town-based retailers.

[DOCUMENT: Textile Sector Business Notes]
Rural textile and tailoring micro-enterprises range from individual tailoring units to small handloom/power-loom operations. Key opportunities exist in uniform stitching contracts (schools, government departments), festival-season demand spikes, and value-added embroidery or block-printing work. Threats include seasonal demand concentration (festivals, wedding season), competition from cheaper factory-made garments sold in nearby town markets, and dependency on raw material (fabric) supply chains that are often not locally sourced.

[DOCUMENT: SWOT Framework for Micro-Enterprises]
A standard SWOT analysis for a rural micro-enterprise should assess: Strengths (owner skill/experience, low overhead costs, local trust/relationships), Weaknesses (limited working capital, lack of formal financial literacy, limited market reach beyond immediate locality), Opportunities (unserved local demand, government scheme support, potential for value addition), and Threats (seasonal demand variation, single-buyer or single-supplier dependency, competition from larger urban-based players, climate/agricultural dependency in rural economies).
